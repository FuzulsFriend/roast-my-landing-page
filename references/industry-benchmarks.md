# Industry Benchmarks

Different business types have different success criteria. A SaaS landing page and an ecommerce product page should NOT be scored the same way. This file defines what matters more (and what matters less) per business type.

---

## Dimension Weight Adjustments by Business Type

The default weights are equal across all types (defined in the scoring rubric). The adjustments below indicate which dimensions deserve **more scrutiny** vs. **more leniency** for each business type.

| Dimension | SaaS | Ecommerce | Course / Info Product | Agency / Service | Newsletter / Lead Magnet |
|-----------|------|-----------|----------------------|-----------------|------------------------|
| First Impression | **High** | **High** | **High** | Medium | Medium |
| Copy and Messaging | **High** | Medium | **Critical** | **High** | **Critical** |
| Call-to-Action | **High** | **Critical** | **High** | Medium | **Critical** |
| Trust and Social Proof | **High** | **Critical** | **Critical** | **Critical** | Medium |
| Mobile and Accessibility | **High** | **Critical** | Medium | Medium | Medium |
| Performance and Technical | **High** | **Critical** | Medium | Medium | Low |

**Legend:**
- **Critical** = This dimension can make or break conversion for this business type. Score strictly.
- **High** = Very important. Score at standard rigor.
- **Medium** = Important but less decisive. Score at standard rigor, don't over-penalize minor issues.
- **Low** = Still check, but minor issues here are less impactful for this business type.

## SaaS Landing Pages

**Primary goal:** Free trial signup, demo booking, or freemium activation

### What matters most
- **Clear value proposition** — Visitors must understand what the tool does in 3 seconds. SaaS products are often abstract ("workflow automation", "data pipeline"), so clarity is everything.
- **How it works** — A 3-step plan section is nearly mandatory. SaaS products need to feel simple.
- **Social proof** — User count, logo bar, testimonials with company names. B2B buyers need validation.
- **CTA friction** — Email-only signup beats 5-field form. Free tier beats "contact sales" for most.
- **Performance** — SaaS visitors are often tech-savvy and will notice slow loads or broken elements.

### What's acceptable
- Minimal social proof for very early stage (pre-launch) — substitute with founder credentials or beta user count
- No pricing page if genuinely custom enterprise pricing (but explain why)
- Technical terminology IF the target audience is technical (a DevOps tool can say "CI/CD")

### What's NOT acceptable
- No free trial, no demo, no self-serve signup — forcing "contact sales" as the only option is a conversion killer for SMB SaaS
- Feature-first copy with no benefits — "Webhooks, SSO, RBAC, API" means nothing without context
- No "how it works" section — visitors leave confused about the product

### Benchmark scores
| Score | What it means for SaaS |
|-------|----------------------|
| 85+ | Competitive with top SaaS landing pages. Likely converting well. |
| 70-84 | Functional but missing key elements. Room to improve conversion significantly. |
| 55-69 | Below average for SaaS. Visitors probably leaving confused. |
| Below 55 | Major problems. The page is actively losing signups. |

## Ecommerce Product Pages

**Primary goal:** Add to cart / Buy now

### What matters most
- **Product visuals** — High-quality images from multiple angles. Video if possible. Zoom capability.
- **Trust signals** — Reviews, star ratings, trust badges (payment security, shipping guarantee), return policy.
- **Mobile experience** — 70%+ of ecommerce traffic is mobile. Mobile experience must be excellent.
- **Performance** — Every additional second of load time reduces ecommerce conversion by ~7%.
- **Price and availability** — Price must be visible, unambiguous, and next to the CTA.

### What's acceptable
- Less narrative copy than SaaS — product pages can be more transactional
- "Buy now" and "Add to cart" as CTA text (visitors expect it in ecommerce)
- Multiple CTAs per page (color variants, size selectors, related products) as long as the primary is clear

### What's NOT acceptable
- No product images or only one low-quality image
- No reviews or ratings (or clearly fake ones)
- Hidden shipping costs or surprise fees at checkout
- No return policy visible on the product page
- Slow image loading (product images MUST be optimized)

### Benchmark scores
| Score | What it means for ecommerce |
|-------|---------------------------|
| 85+ | Strong product page. Trust signals and mobile experience are polished. |
| 70-84 | Decent but likely losing sales to missing trust signals or friction. |
| 55-69 | Significant issues. Visitors are probably abandoning before purchase. |
| Below 55 | The page looks untrustworthy or is broken on mobile. Fix urgently. |

## Course / Information Product Pages

**Primary goal:** Course enrollment, ebook purchase, webinar signup

### What matters most
- **Copy quality** — These pages live or die on copy. The writing must be exceptional, persuasive, and specific.
- **Social proof** — Student results, testimonials with outcomes ("I got a 40% raise after this course"), enrollment numbers.
- **Instructor credibility** — Who is teaching this? Why should I trust them? Credentials and authority signals are critical.
- **Stakes and transformation** — Before/after framing is powerful here. "Before: struggling with X. After: achieving Y."
- **Risk reversal** — Money-back guarantee is almost mandatory for paid courses.

### What's acceptable
- Long-form sales pages (these often outperform short ones for courses priced over $100)
- Multiple CTA buttons down the page (price anchoring, urgency elements near each CTA)
- Countdown timers IF the deadline is real (course enrollment closing, cohort starting)
- Video sales letter (VSL) as the main content

### What's NOT acceptable
- No instructor credentials or bio
- No student testimonials with specific outcomes
- Fake urgency ("Only 3 spots left!" when it's a digital course with no capacity limit)
- Price only revealed after clicking through — this feels like a trap
- No money-back guarantee on a premium-priced course

### Benchmark scores
| Score | What it means for courses |
|-------|-------------------------|
| 85+ | Professional sales page with strong proof. Likely converting well. |
| 70-84 | Good foundation but missing key persuasion elements. |
| 55-69 | Needs work on credibility and copy. Visitors aren't convinced. |
| Below 55 | Page lacks trust signals. Visitors don't believe the transformation is real. |

## Agency / Service Business Pages

**Primary goal:** Contact form submission, discovery call booking, RFP request

### What matters most
- **Portfolio and case studies** — Show, don't tell. Real work with real results.
- **Trust signals** — Client logos, testimonials from decision-makers, industry awards, certifications.
- **Specificity** — "We do marketing" vs. "We build lead generation systems for B2B SaaS companies." Specificity wins.
- **Copy quality** — Agencies sell expertise. If the copy on the landing page is mediocre, visitors will question the agency's capability.
- **Process clarity** — "How we work" section that shows the engagement model (discovery, proposal, execution).

### What's acceptable
- "Book a call" or "Request a proposal" as the primary CTA (agencies often need a conversation first)
- No pricing on the page (custom pricing is standard for agencies)
- Professional but not flashy design (agencies serving conservative industries like finance or law)
- Longer form (company name, budget range, project description) — agency leads need qualifying

### What's NOT acceptable
- No portfolio or case studies at all — visitors need to see the work
- Generic "we do everything" positioning — specialists win over generalists
- Stock photos instead of real team/work photos
- No clear way to get in touch (contact form buried or missing)
- Copy with grammatical errors or poor writing (for an agency that sells communication skills)

### Benchmark scores
| Score | What it means for agencies |
|-------|--------------------------|
| 85+ | Professional, credible, clearly communicates expertise. |
| 70-84 | Good but portfolio or trust signals need strengthening. |
| 55-69 | Looks generic. Not differentiating from competitors. |
| Below 55 | The page undermines credibility. Potential clients will go elsewhere. |

## Newsletter / Lead Magnet Pages

**Primary goal:** Email signup

### What matters most
- **CTA clarity and simplicity** — One field (email), one button. That's it. Any additional friction is fatal.
- **Value proposition in the headline** — What will I get? How often? Why is it worth my email?
- **Copy specificity** — "Weekly newsletter" is weak. "Every Tuesday: 3 actionable SEO tips backed by data" is strong.
- **Sample content** — Show a preview of what subscribers get. Past issues, sample content, or screenshots.
- **Social proof** — Subscriber count, open rate, testimonials from readers.

### What's acceptable
- Minimal design (one-page, single-column, just headline + form + proof)
- No navigation at all (pure squeeze page is fine for lead magnets)
- Urgency if genuine (limited-time lead magnet, cohort-based newsletter)
- Light on mobile/accessibility (simple pages usually pass by default)

### What's NOT acceptable
- Requiring more than email + first name to subscribe
- No indication of content or frequency ("Subscribe to our newsletter" with no detail)
- No sample content or preview of what subscribers get
- No unsubscribe mention (builds distrust)
- Multiple CTAs competing with the signup (social links, blog links, product links)

### Benchmark scores
| Score | What it means for newsletters |
|-------|------------------------------|
| 85+ | Clean, compelling, low-friction. Should convert well. |
| 70-84 | Functional but not optimized. Copy or proof could be stronger. |
| 55-69 | Too much friction or too little value communicated. |
| Below 55 | Visitors have no compelling reason to give their email. |

## How to Apply Benchmarks During the Audit

1. **Ask the business type** in the onboarding questions (before starting analysis)
2. **Adjust scrutiny** per the table above — score "Critical" dimensions more strictly
3. **Reference the appropriate benchmark** when reporting the overall grade
4. **Note what's "acceptable" vs. "not acceptable"** for the business type in the findings
5. **When in doubt,** score at default rigor — the benchmarks are guidelines, not strict overrides

### Cross-Type Pages

Some pages serve multiple purposes (e.g., SaaS with a newsletter component, agency with a product). In this case:
- Ask the user: "What's the ONE thing you most want visitors to do on this page?"
- Score based on that primary goal
- Note secondary goals as context but don't let them dilute the primary scoring

### Early-Stage / Pre-Launch Products

If the user says they're pre-launch, early access, or invite-only:

1. **Trust and Social Proof**: Don't just say "no social proof." Acknowledge it's expected and recommend specific substitutes:
   - Founder bio with relevant credentials
   - Beta user count or waitlist size
   - "Built by [team with X experience]" section
   - Early user quotes (even 2-3 from beta testers)

2. **CTA**: An invite-code gate is acceptable IF clearly communicated. "Get Started" -> invite code form is a friction point to flag, but "Join the waitlist" or "Request early access" is fine.

3. **Numbers/Metrics**: Don't penalize for missing "12,000 users" metrics. Instead check for: items processed, beta feedback received, or progress metrics.

4. **Score adjustment**: Don't lower Trust below 30/100 for early-stage IF they have at least one substitute (founder credentials, beta count, or early testimonial). Below 30 should be reserved for launched products with zero proof.
